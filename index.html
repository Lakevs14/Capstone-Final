00 — Abstract

Overview

Access to transit remains a significant component of transit equity in urban settings, with the spatial distribution of transit infrastructure affecting socioeconomic disparities. This study examines the proximity of transit stops within Denver block groups, and whether those block groups with the least transit accessibility are disproportionately consisting of lower household incomes, higher poverty levels, and higher concentrations of renter households.

Using the Network Analyst Closest Facility tool in ArcGIS Pro and Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm, network-oriented walking distances from building centroids to the nearest stops are generated across Denver block groups with available data from the Denver Open Catalog and American Community Survey. Three tiers of transit accessibility — low, medium, and high — are selected using 15 block group samples each to generate three one-way ANOVA results testing statistical differences across demographic variables.

Statistically significant inverse relationships are discovered between transit accessibility and median household incomes, and a statistically significant positive relationship between accessibility and renter-occupied households. Poverty households did not reach statistical significance. The results imply that Denver's transit network is concentrated in lower-income, higher renter-occupied urban core areas, while suburban, wealthier, automobile-dominant neighborhoods have fewer transit stops. Isolated lower-income neighborhoods with low transit availability need to be prioritized. Implications include a need for further transit-oriented infrastructure in Denver.

01 — Introduction & Background

Research Question & Central Argument

Access to public transportation is a fundamental necessity for social equity, shaping residents' ability to reach employment, healthcare, food, and education. Denver, Colorado — a rapidly growing metropolitan area — faces mounting pressure from suburban expansion beyond existing transit infrastructure.

This study examines the following question: To what extent does block group proximity to public transit stops vary across Denver, and are block groups with the least transit accessibility disproportionately characterized by lower household incomes, higher poverty levels, and greater concentrations of renter-occupied households?

The central argument is that Denver's transit infrastructure is concentrated in the urban core, leaving suburban block groups with the lowest accessibility and greatest automobile dependence. Paradoxically, while transit-dependent populations may currently reside near transit, rising housing costs are displacing lower-income residents toward the periphery — where both housing affordability and mobility suffer. Even where low-income areas retain immediate transit access, employment destinations in peripheral zones frequently do not, compounding mobility barriers.

This pattern reflects broader North American trends. Literature identifies transit-rich corridors as clustering in denser, more racially diverse, and lower-income urban neighborhoods, while affordable housing increasingly concentrates in peripheral zones with minimal transit access (Kramer, 2018). DRCOG acknowledges these imbalances, pursuing redevelopments such as the Cherry Creek Corridor to better align land use and transportation investment.

Key Terms

Transit Accessibility

Network-based walking distances from residential locations to the nearest transit stops, calculated using Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm along pedestrian infrastructure.

Term 02

Block Group

The U.S. Census Bureau's smallest geographic unit with publicly available demographic data, used here as the primary unit of spatial analysis.

Term 03

ANOVA

Analysis of Variance — the statistical method used to assess whether meaningful differences in demographic variables exist across low, medium, and high accessibility groups at p ≤ 0.05.

02 — Literature Review

Three Thematic Frameworks

The literature on transportation equity demonstrates that mobility constraints are inseparable from socioeconomic conditions, and that geographic distribution of transit infrastructure reinforces inequality.

Theme 01

Transportation Disadvantage as a Structural Condition

Kramer (2018) found transit-rich areas concentrated in urban cores across 17 North American metros, with affordable housing in peripheral zones experiencing poor service. Walks (2018) showed automobile-dependent structures force lower-income residents into automotive debt. Lucas (2011) documents how systematic policy and land-use inequalities cause social exclusion in Tshwane, South Africa — limiting access to education and healthcare.

Kramer 2018 · Walks 2018 · Lucas 2011
Theme 02

Health & Welfare Consequences of Transit Inaccessibility

Rajasooriar & Soma (2022) documented food access barriers driven by mobility constraints among nonprofit food hub users in Vancouver. Oluyede et al. (2022) identified transportation barriers preventing healthcare access in North Carolina. Im et al. (2025) found comparable disparities in Atlanta's food-as-medicine programs. Together, these studies link transit inaccessibility to chronic disease management and nutritional outcomes.

Rajasooriar & Soma 2022 · Oluyede et al. 2022 · Im et al. 2025
Theme 03

Transit Investment, Mode Shift & Equity

Yousefzadeh Barri et al. (2021) found lower-income households in Greater Toronto responsive to transit improvements, with accessibility improvements advancing mode shifts and social equity goals. Bhattacharjee & Goetz (2012) assessed Denver's light rail using temporal VMT data from 1992–2008, finding short-term reductions in adjacent highway traffic and slowing congestion growth — with spatially varied outcomes across corridors.

Yousefzadeh Barri et al. 2021 · Bhattacharjee & Goetz 2012
03 — Methods

Spatial Quantitative Methodology

Data Sources

Primary data are derived from the Denver Open Catalog using the residential buildings dataset, combined Sidewalks–Current and Trails & Sidewalks shapefiles for walking paths, and the BTS National Transit Stops dataset. Demographic variables — median household income, poverty counts, and renter-to-total household ratios — are drawn from U.S. Census Bureau ACS block group data joined to the TIGER/Line shapefile.

Network Distance Calculation

Transit accessibility was measured using network-based walking distance from each residential building centroid to its nearest transit stop. Using ArcGIS Pro's Network Analyst Closest Facility tool, Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm calculated these distances along the pedestrian network rather than straight-line Euclidean approximation — an approach consistent with established transportation accessibility research reflecting actual pedestrian travel behavior (Chen, 2020).

Accessibility Tier Classification & Sampling

Block groups were classified into three tiers — low, medium, and high transit accessibility — based on mean network walking distances to the nearest stop. Fifteen block groups were sampled from each tier: those with the highest average distances (low access), moderate distances (medium access), and lowest distances (high access), yielding a total sample of 45 block groups.

Statistical Analysis

Three one-way ANOVA tests were conducted at a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 to test differences across the three tiers for each dependent variable: median household income, poverty count, and renter-to-total household ratio. ANOVA compares group means and is resistant to normality violations across a wide range of sample sizes and distribution shapes (Blanca et al., 2017).

Limitations

Reliance on block group aggregate data may obscure within-group variation and does not capture individual-level travel behavior. Walking distance as the sole accessibility measure excludes transit frequency, service hours, and pedestrian infrastructure quality. Future research should incorporate disaggregate data and qualitative methods to produce a more comprehensive equity analysis.

05 — Discussion & Conclusion

Interpretation, Implications & Future Directions

Finding 01

Income–Accessibility Inverse Relationship

The statistically significant inverse relationship between transit accessibility and median household income aligns with Kramer's (2018) observations across North American metros. Denver's nuance — low-access block groups recording the highest median incomes ($101,362) — suggests these areas include employment destinations that lower-income households must travel to, compounding mobility barriers. Walks' (2018) automobile-debt dynamic explains how higher-income households afford peripheral neighborhoods, while lower-income peripheral residents face compounding disadvantage.

Kramer 2018 · Walks 2018
Finding 02

Renter Concentration & Displacement Risk

The significant positive relationship between transit accessibility and renter concentration reflects market dynamics where transit proximity is valued in rental markets. The equity concern is displacement: as rising housing costs push lower-income renters toward the periphery — particularly affordable growth corridors near DIA — they lose the transit access they previously relied upon. If transit investments remain concentrated in the urban core, populations already with access continue to benefit while displaced residents face compounding challenges.

Kramer 2018 · Yousefzadeh Barri et al. 2021
Finding 03

Non-Significant Poverty Findings

Non-significant poverty results reflect high within-group variance in Denver's mixed-income neighborhoods — consistent with Im et al.'s (2025) conclusion that poverty and transit access share a non-linear relationship shaped by local context. Developments around Sloan's Lake integrating both high- and low-income households may contribute to this pattern. Greater sample sizes may reveal significance that this study's 15-block-group samples could not detect.

Im et al. 2025
Finding 04

Light Rail Corridors & Geographic Equity

Bhattacharjee & Goetz's (2012) documentation of VMT reductions along established Denver light rail corridors aligns with this study's finding that high-access block groups cluster near those corridors. Peripheral block groups with low access have not benefited from mode shifts or congestion reductions — demonstrating the equity implications of geographically concentrated transit investment.

Bhattacharjee & Goetz 2012

Policy Recommendations

  • → Transit-Oriented Development (TOD)Integrate affordable housing with high-quality transit service in underserved peripheral corridors, prioritizing block groups identified in the choropleth analysis as highest-need (Kramer, 2018).
  • → Subsidized Fares & ParatransitImplement subsidized transit passes and paratransit improvements for transit-dependent populations — particularly those in low-access block groups where low-income households face compounding health and food access barriers (Rajasooriar & Soma, 2022; Oluyede et al., 2022).
  • → Light Rail & BRT ExtensionsStrategic extensions of light rail or bus rapid transit into underserved suburban corridors, guided by the spatial distribution of low-access, lower-income block groups. Yousefzadeh Barri et al. (2021) demonstrate that lower-income households respond positively to such improvements (Bhattacharjee & Goetz, 2012).
  • → Pedestrian Infrastructure & Land UseAddress communities' needs beyond transit infrastructure — ensuring land uses support pedestrian access, reliable service hours, and fare structures that do not create financial barriers for the populations most dependent on transit.

Future Research Directions

Future research should expand sample sizes, incorporate transit quality metrics beyond distance (frequency, service hours, pedestrian infrastructure quality), and apply temporal analysis to track how investments shift demographic patterns over time. Qualitative interviews with residents in low-access block groups would contextualize the statistical findings and capture lived transportation disadvantages — building the comprehensive, mixed-methods understanding necessary for equitable and informed planning decisions in Denver.
